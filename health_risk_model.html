💡 The Problem

Noncommunicable diseases (diabetes, hypertension, stroke) are leading causes of death worldwide. Most prediction systems rely on expensive laboratory tests, limiting accessibility, and are built separately from hospital resource management — even though patients arriving too late (or unnecessarily) is what actually overloads hospitals.

🎯 Our Solution

A system with three integrated parts: (1) a hierarchical, two-layer machine-learning pipeline that screens and then scores individual disease risk from non-laboratory data, (2) disease-specific benchmark classifiers trained on real public health datasets to validate what's achievable, and (3) a distance-and-capacity-aware optimization algorithm that routes at-risk patients to hospitals.

🔬 Three Real-Dataset Benchmark Models

Diabetes (BRFSS 2015, 250K+ records): XGBoost + calibration, 85.2% accuracy, ROC-AUC 0.822
Hypertension (Miadul dataset): Leakage-free XGBoost, 98% accuracy vs. 82% logistic-regression baseline
Stroke (Kaggle, 5,110 records): XGBoost + SMOTE, 95% accuracy, ROC-AUC 0.796

🧬 Synthetic Data Innovation

Generated 30,000 medically-plausible synthetic patient records using composite statistical distributions (normal + exponential glucose, gamma-shifted blood pressure, beta-distributed BMI) to realistically capture outliers, not just typical cases.

🏥 Clinical-First Design

Three-tier "traffic light" risk stratification (Green/Yellow/Red), 0–100 percentile risk scoring, and dynamic per-disease threshold calibration tuned to keep recall of at-risk patients above 95%.

🚑 Distance + Capacity Hospital Routing

An optimization algorithm that weighs travel distance, hospital capacity, and risk urgency — from a 95%-distance-weighted "Golden Hour" rule for stroke down to a 25%-weighted, load-balancing rule for low-risk cases.

🚦 Risk Stratification System

🟢 Low Risk

No immediate action needed. Annual check-ups recommended. Distance to hospital weighted just 25% — load-balancing prioritized.

🟡 Moderate Risk

Lifestyle changes advised. Follow-up within 3-6 months. Routing balances distance (60%) against hospital workload.

🔴 High Risk

Immediate medical consultation. Distance weighted 95% — closest capable hospital wins, per the "Golden Hour" principle.

🔬 Finding and Fixing Data Leakage

One of our most important debugging stories: an early hypertension model that looked almost too good — because it was cheating.

⚠️ The Leak

Our first hypertension dataset included BP_History and Medication fields. Both are direct outcomes of already having hypertension, not risk factors that precede it — so the model was essentially being handed the answer. It scored high, but would have been useless for screening people who haven't been diagnosed yet.

✅ After Removing Leakage (XGBoost)

  • Trained only on pre-diagnosis lifestyle & behavioral features
  • 98% accuracy, 97% recall on the held-out test set
  • Salt intake converted to Low/Moderate/High percentile bins so it's answerable without lab data
  • Tuned via 5-fold GridSearchCV with max depth capped at 3 to resist overfitting on ~2,000 rows

Baseline for Comparison (Logistic Regression)

  • Same leakage-free feature set
  • 82% accuracy — a fair, non-leaking benchmark
  • Confirms the XGBoost gain is real signal, not memorized answers
  • Kept as an interpretable fallback comparison

🎯 Critical Insight

A high accuracy number means nothing until you've checked which features earned it. Removing two "obvious" columns was the difference between a model that memorizes diagnoses and one that could actually screen someone before they're diagnosed.

Dynamic Threshold Calibration

Rather than picking thresholds by hand, we trained a reference model on all 27 features and swept the screening model's percentile output in 1-point increments, plotting recall (at-risk patients correctly flagged) against referral load. Each disease's threshold is the point where recall first exceeds 95% — capturing almost every at-risk patient while keeping the number of people forwarded to specialist models manageable.

Why Min-Max Instead of CDF Normalization

Our first approach used cumulative-distribution normalization, but it compressed extreme risk cases into the 70–80th percentile — hiding exactly the patients who needed the highest scores. Switching to min-max scaling ((raw - min) / (max - min) × 100) restored a full 0–100 severity gradient.

🧬 Synthetic Data Pipeline

To train the screening and specialist regressors, we built a synthetic data generator that went through two iterations to better mirror real patient variability, including outliers.

📊 30,000 Patient Records

10 CSV files across all/important/disease-specific feature sets, each in a v1 (basic normal distributions) and v2 (composite, outlier-aware distributions) version.

🔬 Composite Distributions (v2)

Glucose: normal + exponential blend (60–450 mg/dL). Blood pressure: gamma-distributed stress factor over a normal base (90–240 mmHg). BMI: beta distribution scaled to 15–60, right-skewed like real populations. Age range widened to 15–95.

✅ Edge & Adversarial Case Testing

Validated the pipeline against edge cases (single unambiguous disease), close cases (overlapping symptoms), and adversarial cases (deliberately misleading labels) — the models tracked true data patterns rather than mislabeled shortcuts.

🚑 Hospital Routing & Resource Allocation

The "Resource Allocation" half of the project: once a patient is risk-scored, an optimization algorithm decides which hospital to route them to, balancing proximity against hospital workload.

Distance Calculation

Patients and hospitals are modeled as objects with (x, y) coordinates. Distance uses a fast Euclidean approximation (‖location_patient − location_hospital‖ × 111 to convert degrees to km), then normalized against MAX_DIST_ANKARA = 70 km so distance can't mathematically overwhelm other factors.

Risk-Dependent Distance Weighting

95% Red / Critical

Nearest capable hospital wins — the "Golden Hour" principle for stroke and other critical cases.

60% Yellow / Urgent but Stable

Balances travel distance against expected hospital waiting time.

25% Green / Low Risk

Prioritizes routing to a hospital with lighter workload over the closest one.

Hospital Ranking & Fallback

The final ranking algorithm combines distance (35%), hospital capacity (35%), and risk match (30%) to generate ranked hospital recommendations. If no specialized hospital is available for a case, the system automatically falls back to the nearest facility as an emergency option, and logs every calculated distance and rating for review.

🧪 Real-World Validation & Ethics Approval

Every model here was trained on synthetic or existing public datasets. To validate the system against real people, we designed a unified survey and secured formal ethics approval before collecting any data.

✅ Ethics Committee Approval

Submitted to the AYBU Health Sciences Ethics Committee on 9 Jan 2026; approved 22 Jan 2026 (Decision No. 01/26), covering informed consent, participant anonymization via random IDs, and a five-year encrypted data retention policy.

📋 54-Question Survey

A single Google Forms questionnaire, split into 7 thematic sections, consolidates all feature variables needed across the diabetes, hypertension, and stroke models into one respondent-friendly instrument.

📐 Power-Analysis-Backed Sample Sizes

Using Cochran's formula and G*Power (95% confidence, 5% margin of error, Turkey-specific disease prevalence, +10% buffer for incomplete responses): 1,258 respondents for hypertension, 662 for diabetes, and 2,043 for stroke.

🎓 Key Lessons Learned

🕵️ Audit Features Before Trusting Accuracy

A too-good hypertension model turned out to be leaking diagnosis outcomes back in as inputs. High accuracy is only meaningful once you've verified every feature could plausibly be known before diagnosis.

⚖️ Calibration Is a Double-Edged Sword

Probability calibration made our risk scores more reliable overall, but for the heavily imbalanced stroke dataset it unexpectedly suppressed recall for the positive class — a clinically important trade-off we're still resolving.

📐 Normalization Choice Changes Clinical Meaning

CDF-based normalization compressed our highest-risk patients into the 70-80th percentile. Switching to min-max scaling was necessary to preserve a true 0-100 severity gradient for decision-making.

🎯 Data-Driven Thresholds Beat Guesses

Sweeping the screening threshold against recall/load curves — rather than picking 50% by default — let each disease get its own empirically justified cutoff for routing to specialist models.
